How to choose an AI tool

There are thousands of AI tools now, and a new one launches every day. Choosing badly rarely costs much money — free tiers see to that — but it costs time, and time spent learning a tool you abandon is gone. This short method will get you to a good decision in about an hour.

1. Name the job, not the category

"I need an AI tool" is not a requirement. "I need to turn my rambling voice notes into clean meeting summaries" is. Write down the single job you want done, the input you will feed in, and what a good output looks like. A tool that is famous for the wrong job is still the wrong tool. This one sentence will eliminate 90% of your options immediately, which is exactly what you want.

2. Start with a generalist, then specialise

If a big general assistant — ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini — does your job well enough, stop there. Generalists improve fast, cost less than a stack of niche subscriptions, and save you learning five interfaces. Move to a specialist only when you can name the specific thing the generalist fails at: garbled text in images, weak video motion, robotic voices. Specialists earn their place by fixing a named failure, not by being trendy.

3. Test with your real work, not the demo

Every tool looks brilliant on its own examples. Instead, take three real tasks from your actual week — the messier the better — and run them through your top two or three candidates. Judge the outputs side by side on accuracy, how much editing they need, and how the tool feels after the novelty wears off. Twenty minutes of honest testing beats two hours of reviews, because your work is not the reviewer's work.

4. Check the costs that hide behind "free"

Free tiers are real, but they have edges: daily limits, watermarks, lower-quality models, and your data possibly being used for training. Before committing, find the pricing page and answer three questions. What exactly runs out on the free plan? What does the first paid tier cost per month, honestly, including per-seat pricing? And can you export your work if you leave? A tool that traps your files is charging you rent, not a fee.

5. Mind the boring risks

Two checks people skip. First, privacy: if the tool will see client data, health details, or unreleased work, read its data policy or use a business tier with training opt-outs. Second, longevity: AI startups fold constantly, so prefer tools with visible traction — real users, regular updates, a company that answers support email. Your workflow should not depend on a tool that may vanish by spring.

6. Commit for a month, then review

Pick the winner and use it properly for a month before judging. Most AI tools have a learning curve measured in days, and switching weekly means you pay that curve forever without reaching the payoff. After a month you will know, from experience rather than marketing, whether it earned a permanent place — and whether it deserves your money.
